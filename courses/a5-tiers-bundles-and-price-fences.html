Learn Pricing Analytics with Phoebe · Analyst session 5 of 6

Tiers, bundles and price fences

One price for everyone leaves money with the customers who would have paid more and loses the customers who would only pay less. Tiers, fences and bundles are how a business charges different people different prices without asking anyone what they would pay. Tonight builds a three-step ladder for Kettle & Co's tea, draws the fence that keeps its segments apart, prices a bundle two ways, then breaks a real month of revenue growth into price, volume and mix on Olist marketplace rows from Black Friday 2017. Kettle numbers are constructed; the Olist rows are real.

Part 0

Where we start

Sessions 1 to 4 treated a product as one price. Real price lists are ladders: a small size, a big size, a subscription, a bundle, a student rate. Each rung is a guess about who will pick it. Tonight is about building those rungs so that the right people pick the right one, and about the analyst's half of the job afterwards: when revenue moves, saying how much came from prices, how much from more items sold, and how much from customers shifting between rungs. The theory of why versioning works lives in Economics, pricing with the number; here we build and measure.

Part 1 · covers good-better-best

Three versions, and customers sort themselves

5 min live

Good-better-best puts three versions on the shelf at three prices and lets each customer pick. Nobody is asked what they would pay; the choice reveals it. The ladder works when every step is a fair deal for the people it is built for and a worse deal for everyone else. Kettle's ladder below is a constructed example: the 100 g tin, a 250 g caddy, and a subscription of three tins a month.

LiveWhat each tier is for3 min▶

The arithmetic first. Constructed Kettle & Co example. The tin: 1,800 yen for 100 g, unit cost 720 yen, margin 1,080 yen (60%). The caddy: 3,900 yen for 250 g, so 3,900 / 2.5 = 1,560 yen per 100 g, which is 240 yen less than the tin: 240 / 1,800 = 13.3% cheaper per gram. Assume the caddy costs 1,600 yen to fill and pack: margin 3,900 - 1,600 = 2,300 yen a caddy, or 2,300 / 2.5 = 920 yen per 100 g (59.0% of price). The subscription: three tins for 4,860 yen, so 4,860 / 3 = 1,620 yen a tin, and 1,620 / 1,800 = 0.90, 10% off. Cost 3 x 720 = 2,160; margin 4,860 - 2,160 = 2,700 a month, 900 yen per 100 g.

The tin is the reference. It sets the price per gram everyone compares against and serves the occasional drinker who would never finish 250 g while it is fresh.

The caddy's job. It catches the customer who would have bought two tins: two tins cost 3,600 yen for 200 g, and for 300 yen more the caddy gives 250 g. Margin per gram falls from 1,080 to 920, but margin per sale rises from 1,080 (one tin) or 2,160 (two tins) to 2,300. Next to the tin, the caddy looks like the obvious deal; that comparison is the middle option doing its work. Whether a given middle option pulls choices on your customers is an empirical question, not a law: test it with a proper A/B test.

The subscription's job. It is not the cheapest per gram (1,620 against the caddy's 1,560). It sells freshness and not having to remember, and it buys Kettle three months of predictable demand. Its value is retention, measured with customer lifetime value, not margin per gram.

Self-studyTiers and the value stack2 min▶

Each tier needs its own price to sit below its own economic value to the customer it is built for: the price of the next best alternative plus what makes this version better, minus what makes it worse. That stack is built in Business session 2, value, cost or competitor. For the caddy buyer the next best alternative is two or three tins; for the subscriber it is remembering to reorder, or a supermarket tea that is always on the shelf. If a tier's price climbs above its own value stack, its customers drop down a step, and the ladder collapses into one rung.

Why charging different prices for versions works at all, and when it is price discrimination in the economist's sense, is taught in Economics, pricing with the number. Read it there; the ladder above is the analyst's working version.

Real world

The two-tin customers. Before the caddy, Kettle's order data showed a cluster of baskets with exactly two tins. Those customers were already telling the business they drank more than 100 g between orders. The caddy was priced for them: a little more than two tins, a lot more tea. The question for the next review was not "did caddy sales rise?" but "did two-tin baskets fall, and did margin per order rise?". Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Part 2 · covers price fences and bundles

A fence must cost more to climb than it saves

5 min live

A price fence is a rule that lets one segment get a lower price without letting everyone else cross over to it. Time is a fence (an off-peak rate), so is quantity (the caddy), channel (wholesale for cafes, online for homes), features (a plain tin and a gift tin) and customer type (a student rate with a student card). Every fence passes one test: for the customer who would pay full price, crossing it must cost more than the discount is worth. If it does not, the fence leaks and the full-price customers walk through it.

LiveA fence that leaks3 min▶

The fence. Kettle's 1 kg catering bag lists at 9,600 yen for cafes, grocers and hotels: 9,600 / 10 = 960 yen per 100 g, against 1,800 yen per 100 g for the tin online. The fence is the channel: the bag is sold only to registered business accounts. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

The leak. One cafe starts weighing the bag into paper pouches for regulars. For a home drinker, crossing the fence used to mean opening a business account; now it means asking at the counter. The cost of climbing dropped to almost nothing, while the discount stayed at 840 yen per 100 g (1,800 - 960). Online tin sales near that cafe fall and nobody sees why, because the wholesale invoice looks normal.

The analyst's check. Fences leak quietly, so watch for the symptom: one segment's volume rising while its neighbour's falls in the same place and the same weeks. The repair raises the cost of climbing again (a resale clause, a pack that only makes sense in a kitchen) rather than cutting the online price.

Self-studyPure vs mixed bundles3 min▶

Two kinds. A pure bundle sells the items only together. A mixed bundle sells them separately and together, with the bundle a little cheaper than the sum. Constructed Kettle & Co example: the tin at 1,800 yen (margin 1,080), a glass infuser at 1,200 yen (assume cost 400, margin 800), and a tin-plus-infuser bundle at 2,700 yen, which is 3,000 - 300, 10% off the pair. Bundle cost 720 + 400 = 1,120, margin 2,700 - 1,120 = 1,580.

Three customers, constructed values. A values the tin at 2,000 and the infuser at 1,000. B values them at 1,600 and 1,300. C values the tin at 2,200 and has no use for an infuser. Each buys whatever leaves the biggest gap between value and price, if any gap is positive.

★ Three ways to sell, workedseparately only (1,800 and 1,200): A tin (2,000 > 1,800), B infuser (1,300 > 1,200), C tin revenue 1,800 + 1,200 + 1,800 = 4,800 · margin 1,080 + 800 + 1,080 = 2,960 pure bundle only (2,700): A buys (3,000), B buys (2,900), C does not (2,200) revenue 2 x 2,700 = 5,400 · margin 2 x 1,580 = 3,160 mixed (all three on sale): A bundle (gap 300 beats tin's 200), B bundle (gap 200 beats infuser's 100), C tin revenue 2,700 + 2,700 + 1,800 = 7,200 · margin 1,580 + 1,580 + 1,080 = 4,240

The lesson. The pure bundle won A and B but lost C, who wanted only tea. The mixed bundle keeps C on the separate price and still moves A and B to the bundle. A pure bundle makes sense only when hardly anyone wants one item alone. With real data, the inputs are not three made-up values but the share of baskets that already hold both items; Kettle would read that from order lines before choosing.

Real world

The student rate nobody checked. Kettle offered a student code at a university fair and printed it on the flyer. Within a month the code was on a coupon site, and the "student" segment was mostly regular customers. A customer-type fence with no card check is not a fence. The rate went back behind a student email sign-up, which put the cost of climbing back where it belonged. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Part 3 · covers the price-volume-mix bridge

Price, volume and mix: three effects that add up

5 min live

When revenue moves, the first question from a commercial lead is "was it the prices?". The price-volume-mix bridge answers it by splitting the change into three effects: what price changes did, what selling more items did, and what the shift between cheaper and dearer products did. Here it runs on real Olist marketplace rows (Brazilian e-commerce, delivered orders, prices in BRL) for October to November 2017, the month that holds Black Friday. The same idea as a margin bridge in an FP&A pack is taught in Ship Platform Diagnosis, follow the margin; tonight is the pricing version on product rows.

LiveThree effects that add up4 min▶

The rows. 112 products sold in both October and November 2017: 832 items in October, 1,397 in November, so 1,397 - 832 = 565 more items.

Price effect. For each product, (November price - October price) x November units, summed. It answers "what did the price changes cost or earn on what we actually sold?". Here: -R$ 4,534.68.

Volume effect. (November items - October items) x October's average price, where October's average price is R$ 90,798.12 / 832 items. It answers "what would the extra items have earned at last month's average?". Here: +R$ 61,659.78.

Mix effect. The rest: the revenue change minus price minus volume. It is what happened because the items sold shifted between cheaper and dearer products. Here: -R$ 12,904.74, so November's extra items leaned towards cheaper products.

★ The check, alwaysrevenue change: 135,018.48 - 90,798.12 = 44,220.36 effects: -4,534.68 + 61,659.78 - 12,904.74 = 44,220.36

The reading. November grew on volume. Prices fell slightly, which fits Black Friday falling on Friday 24 November 2017, and the mix shifted toward cheaper products. The panel is monthly, so Black Friday is one day inside the November row: the bridge cannot say which day the items sold.

Self-studyWhy order matters3 min▶

When price and volume both move, part of the change belongs to both at once: the price change times the change in units. Whichever effect is computed first at the new quantities absorbs that piece. Change the order and the split moves; the total does not. Constructed Kettle & Co example, one product:

★ One product, two orders, same totaltin: 1,800 yen x 100 tins = 180,000 -> 1,620 yen x 150 tins = 243,000 change: 243,000 - 180,000 = 63,000 order 1, price at NEW units: price -180 x 150 = -27,000 volume +50 x 1,800 = +90,000 total 63,000 order 2, price at OLD units: price -180 x 100 = -18,000 volume +50 x 1,620 = +81,000 total 63,000 the 9,000 that moved: -180 x 50 = -9,000 (the shared piece)

With many products, the same thing happens to the mix effect, because it is defined as the remainder. Neither order is wrong. The rules: pick one order, write it beside the chart, keep it the same every month, and never compare a bridge built one way with a bridge built another way. The widget on this page computes price at November units and volume at October's average price.

Real world

Black Friday on real rows. On the 112 Olist products sold in both months, revenue rose from R$ 90,798.12 to R$ 135,018.48. A headline of "Black Friday discounts drove growth" credits the prices; the bridge says the price changes themselves took R$ 4,534.68 away, and the growth came from selling 565 more items. Whether those extra items were caused by the price cuts or by the season is a different question, and the one Analyst session 4 answers with a comparison group. Real world

Revenue up after the caddy, and not for the reason on the slide. Constructed Kettle & Co example. A month after the caddy settled in, sales moved from 900 tins and 100 caddies to 700 tins and 300 caddies, at unchanged prices. Revenue: 900 x 1,800 + 100 x 3,900 = 2,010,000 yen, then 700 x 1,800 + 300 x 3,900 = 2,430,000 yen, up 420,000. Items: 1,000 both months, so volume is zero, price is zero, and the whole rise is mix. Counted in grams instead, tea sold rose from 115,000 g to 145,000 g, about 26% more. The bridge depends on the unit you count: say which one you used.

Homework

Before Analyst session 6

30 min
  1. Draw your own ladder. For one product family, write each tier's price, price per unit of what customers actually consume, and margin per unit and per sale.
  2. List your fences by type (time, quantity, channel, features, customer type) and, for each, write what crossing it costs a full-price customer against the discount on the other side.
  3. Count the baskets that already hold two items you might bundle, and the baskets that hold only one of them. Decide pure or mixed from those counts.
  4. Build one month's bridge on your own rows: price at the new units, volume at the old average price, mix as the rest, products in only one month listed outside. Check the addition.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What must a price fence do to work?

A fence lets one segment pay less without letting the others follow. If crossing is cheaper than the discount, full-price customers walk through it, as the cafe reselling catering bags showed.

2 · In the bridge, what does the mix effect measure?

A is the price effect, C the volume effect, and B sits outside the bridge. On the Olist rows the mix was -R$ 12,904.74: November's extra items leaned toward cheaper products.

3 · Why does the price-volume-mix split depend on the order you compute it in?

In the one-tin example, the shared piece of -9,000 yen moved between price and volume while the total stayed 63,000. Pick an order, write it down, and keep it.
