Part 1 · covers the order-line grain and where each price lives

One row per order line, five prices on it

5 min live

The grain is the order line: one product, one customer, one order. On that row sit the list price, the on-invoice discounts that turn it into the invoice price, the off-invoice items that turn the invoice into the pocket price, and the unit cost. The order system holds only the first half. The rest lives in a rebate ledger, finance, the marketing budget and logistics, and has to be joined or allocated back to the line.

LiveThe columns, and the rule for each3 min▶

list_price. The price on the price list on the order date, per unit. Store the list version, not today's list: a list that changed mid-year makes every old row look discounted.

on_invoice_*. One column per discount type that prints on the invoice: volume tier, promotional allowance, any one-off exception. invoice_price = list_price minus their sum.

off_invoice_*. One column per item that leaves after the invoice: rebate, early-payment discount, co-op marketing, freight the company absorbs. Each comes from its own system, usually per account per period, so it is allocated to lines by units. pocket_price = invoice_price minus their sum.

unit_cost. Cost to make and deliver one unit, from the cost system, for contribution margin. Freight already counted as off-invoice must not be counted again here.

Self-studyAllocation choices change the answer2 min▶

A quarterly rebate paid to an account has no order id. Spread it by units and every line from that account loses the same yen per bag; spread it by revenue and the cheaper lines lose less. Neither is wrong, but they give different pocket prices per product. Write the rule down next to the table, keep it the same quarter to quarter, and say which one you used when you show a band. Marn and Rosiello's 1992 article calls the full cascade the pocket price waterfall; Business session 3 reads it for decisions.

Real world

The freight nobody owned. Kettle's wholesale team quoted "list less our usual 5%" and the invoice agreed. Logistics paid for delivery on small hotel orders out of its own budget, and nobody joined that cost back to the order. Allocated per bag, freight turned out to be the biggest single off-invoice item in the waterfall: 714 yen a bag on average. Constructed Kettle & Co example; the widget below prints the number.

Part 2 · covers the unit-weighted waterfall

Rows to a waterfall: weight by units

5 min live

The waterfall is a set of averages, one per step, and every average is per unit. Sum each discount times the units on its line, divide by total units, and the steps add up exactly from list to pocket. Average the per-order prices without weights and a one-bag order counts as much as a 40-bag order, and the steps stop adding up.

LiveThe query, in words and in SQL4 min▶

In words. For each column, sum (amount per bag x bags) over all lines, divide by total bags. List minus the two on-invoice averages is the invoice price; minus the four off-invoice averages is the pocket price. Because every average shares the same denominator, the steps add up exactly.

★ The waterfall, unit-weightedSELECT SUM(units * list_price) / SUM(units) AS list_avg, SUM(units * on_inv_volume) / SUM(units) AS volume_disc, SUM(units * on_inv_promo) / SUM(units) AS promo_allow, SUM(units * off_inv_rebate) / SUM(units) AS rebate, SUM(units * off_inv_terms) / SUM(units) AS early_pay, SUM(units * off_inv_coop) / SUM(units) AS coop, SUM(units * off_inv_freight) / SUM(units) AS freight, SUM(units * pocket_price) / SUM(units) AS pocket_avg, SUM(units * pocket_price) / SUM(units * list_price) AS realisation FROM order_lines WHERE product = 'catering_bag_1kg' AND order_date >= '2026-04-01' AND order_date < '2026-10-01';

Realisation. Pocket over list, unit-weighted: 7,665 / 9,600 = 79.8% for Kettle. It is the single number that tells a commercial lead how much of the price list survives.

Self-studyWhy the unweighted version drifts2 min▶

Freight is a fixed cost per delivery spread over the bags in it: small orders carry more freight per bag. An unweighted average of per-order freight treats a 3-bag hotel order like a 40-bag cafe order and overstates freight per bag; the pocket price it implies is lower than the money actually kept per bag. The rule: every step of the waterfall is SUM(units x amount) / SUM(units) over the same rows and the same period. Check it by confirming list minus all steps equals the pocket average to the yen.

Part 3 · covers price realisation and the pocket price band

The band, and the volume trap

5 min live

An average pocket price hides the spread. Compute the pocket price per account and line them up: the range from lowest to highest is the pocket price band. Invoice prices sit in a tight band because the price list is visible; pocket prices spread out because the off-invoice items are not. And the cheapest pocket prices do not always go to the biggest customers.

LiveReading a band3 min▶

Width. Kettle's pocket band runs from 6,371 to 8,986 yen a bag, 2,615 yen wide, against an invoice band 1,102 yen wide. Marn and Rosiello report bands from 35% to 500% of the lowest price across the industries they studied; any band you find is worth reading account by account.

The volume trap. On average the five largest accounts pay a lower pocket price (7,402 yen) than the five smallest (8,328). That is the pattern volume discounts are meant to produce. The dots say something else as well: a 49-bag cafe sits at 6,626 yen, almost as low as the 164-bag cafe at the bottom. Its leak is co-op money and freight on small drops, not volume.

Next question. For each outlier, which step of the waterfall made it an outlier? That is a per-account waterfall: the same query grouped by account.

Self-studyBands on real data need care2 min▶

Compare like with like: one product, one pack size, one period, one currency. Mixing pack sizes makes a band out of nothing; mixing periods turns a list price change into a fake spread. Exclude free samples and returns, or report them as their own step. When the band is wide, the first question for the commercial team is not "who is cheapest?" but "was that price agreed by someone who could see the pocket number?"

Real world

The cafe that looked loyal. Kettle's account review praised a small cafe as a full-price customer: its invoice sat near list. Its pocket price was one of the lowest on the sheet, because it received co-op money for a window display and twice-weekly deliveries of three bags each. The fix was a minimum drop size, not a price change. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

★ Step 5 · band per accountSELECT account_id, SUM(units) AS bags, SUM(units * invoice_price) / SUM(units) AS invoice_avg, SUM(units * pocket_price) / SUM(units) AS pocket_avg FROM order_lines WHERE product = 'catering_bag_1kg' GROUP BY account_id ORDER BY pocket_avg;

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What is the pocket price?

A is the invoice price. The pocket price is what the company keeps after everything that leaves later, which is why it needs data from systems beyond the order system.

2 · Why weight every waterfall step by units?

Unweighted, per-order averages over-count small orders, where freight per bag is largest, and the steps stop reconciling.

3 · Kettle's invoice band is 1,102 yen wide and its pocket band 2,615. What does that tell you?

The invoice band is the visible part. The off-invoice items more than double the spread, and they are not tied cleanly to volume: a 49-bag cafe sits near the bottom.
