Learn Pricing Analytics with Phoebe · Business session 2 of 4

Value, cost or competitor

Every price is pulled by three anchors: what it costs you, what rivals charge, and what the product is worth to the person buying it. Cost tells you where you lose money; it says nothing about what the buyer would pay. Tonight draws the three anchors on one line, builds the economic value stack from the buyer's next best alternative up, and shows the two ways cost-plus pricing quietly loses: the gap it leaves under the value ceiling, and the spiral it starts when volume falls. Kettle & Co and every yen on this page are constructed.

Part 0

Where we start

Business session 1 showed what a price move does to profit once a price exists. Tonight steps back to where the price comes from. Most companies use one of three methods without naming it: mark up the cost, look at the shelf next to you, or work out what the buyer gains. Each answers a different question, and only one of them answers "what would this customer pay?". Kettle & Co, the constructed tea brand selling online in Japan, has a 100 g tin at 1,800 yen with a unit cost of 720 yen. We will price it three ways and see where each method puts it.

Part 1 · covers cost-plus, competitor-based and value-based pricing

Three anchors pull on one price

5 min live

Cost-plus takes the unit cost and adds a markup. Competitor-based takes rivals' prices as the reference and sits above, at or below them. Value-based asks what the customer gets compared with the next best thing they could buy. They are not rival philosophies so much as three different anchors: cost sets the floor you must not go under, value sets the ceiling the buyer will not go over, and competitors sit beside you as a reference. The price has to live between the floor and the ceiling.

LiveThree anchors, one price3 min▶

Cost-plus. Kettle's 100 g tin costs 720 yen to make, pack and ship. A 50% markup gives 720 x 1.5 = 1,080 yen. Fast, defensible in a meeting, and blind to the buyer: the same tin would get the same price whether customers loved it or barely noticed it.

Competitor-based. Constructed rival premium tins sell at 1,500 to 1,700 yen. "Price with the market" would put Kettle somewhere in that band. It tells you where the buyer's eyes already are, but it assumes Kettle's tin is worth the same as theirs.

Value-based. Ask what the tin is worth to the buyer compared with what they would buy instead. Part 2 builds that number for Kettle: 2,100 yen. Kettle charges 1,800 today, above the rivals and well above cost-plus, and still under the ceiling.

The rule of thumb. Cost is a floor, never a target. Value is the ceiling. Competitor prices are evidence about the ceiling, not the ceiling itself. All numbers here are constructed Kettle & Co examples.

Self-studyWhere competitor pricing helps and where it misleads2 min▶

Where it helps. Rival prices are the cheapest evidence you have about what buyers already accept. When products really are close substitutes, they bound the ceiling tightly: price far above them and buyers notice. They also tell you where a new product will be compared, which is half of positioning.

Where it misleads. It copies rivals' mistakes along with their prices; nobody knows whether the rival priced on value or on a guess. It assumes your product is worth what theirs is, erasing exactly the differences you invested in. It can start a price war when every seller anchors on every other. And it hides the real next best alternative: for many Kettle buyers the thing they would buy instead is not another premium tin but supermarket loose-leaf, which Part 2 uses.

Use competitor prices as one input to the value stack (they are often the reference value), never as the answer.

Real world

The 150 yen cut that nobody asked for. A new Kettle sales lead lined up the rival tins at 1,500 to 1,700 yen and proposed moving the 100 g tin from 1,800 to 1,650 "to sit in the market". The finance partner asked one question: what do our buyers say they would drink instead? Twenty subscriber calls later the answer was not a rival tin at all, and the value stack put the ceiling well above 1,800. The cut would have given away 150 yen a tin on a 1,080 yen contribution with no evidence any buyer wanted it. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Part 2 · covers economic value to the customer

The value stack, from the next best alternative up

5 min live

Nagle and Mueller's The Strategy and Tactics of Pricing (6th edition) builds the value ceiling as economic value, reported here in paraphrase: start from the reference value, the price of the buyer's next best alternative; add what makes your product better for this buyer (positive differentiation value); subtract what makes it worse (negative differentiation value). The total is the most a well-informed buyer should pay. Price below it and the buyer has a reason to switch.

LiveBuilding the value stack3 min▶

Reference value: 1,300 yen. For a home tea drinker, the next best alternative to Kettle's tin is a premium loose-leaf 100 g from the supermarket at 1,300 yen. Not the cheapest tea in the shop, and not a rival's tin: the thing this buyer would actually buy if Kettle disappeared.

Positive differentiation: +600 and +400. Freshness, a harvest-dated single estate the supermarket cannot match, is worth 600 yen to this buyer. The subscription that arrives before the caddy runs out is worth 400.

Negative differentiation: -200. The supermarket hands the tea over today; Kettle makes you wait for delivery and asks for a minimum order. That costs the buyer 200 yen of value.

Economic value. 1,300 + 600 + 400 - 200 = 2,100 yen. Kettle's price, 1,800 yen, sits 300 yen below it: that 300 is the buyer's reason to switch from the supermarket. Price at 2,100 and a well-informed buyer is indifferent; price above it and they should stay where they are. Constructed Kettle & Co example; the structure is Nagle and Mueller's, reported.

Self-studyValue is per segment2 min▶

The stack above belongs to one buyer: a home drinker whose alternative is supermarket loose-leaf. A hotel buying for its breakfast room has a different next best alternative, a catering supplier's bulk tea priced per cup, and different differentiators: consistency across thousands of cups and a supplier who invoices monthly matter more than a harvest date. Same tea, different reference value, different stack, different ceiling.

That is why one value number for "the market" is usually wrong. Build a stack per segment, and expect the ceilings to differ. Charging each segment closer to its own ceiling is versioning and price discrimination, which Economics covers with the elasticity in hand: Economics, pricing with the number ↗. Analyst session 5 shows the fences that keep segments in their own tier.

Real world

The question that moved the reference value. Kettle's first value stack started from rival tins at 1,600 yen and came out at 2,400. It looked generous until the team asked twenty subscribers what they bought before Kettle: seventeen named supermarket loose-leaf. Rebuilt from 1,300 yen, the ceiling fell to 2,100, still above 1,800 but with far less room than the first draft promised. The differentiators did not change; the starting point did. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Part 3 · covers why cost-plus leaves money on the table

Two ways cost-plus loses

5 min live

Cost-plus feels safe because it never prices below cost. Its two failures are quieter. First, it ignores the ceiling: whatever the buyer would have paid above the markup stays in the buyer's pocket. Second, it is circular: the unit cost it marks up includes fixed cost spread over the volume you expect, so when volume falls the cost per unit rises, cost-plus raises the price, and volume falls again.

LiveTwo ways cost-plus loses3 min▶

Loss one: the gap under the ceiling. Marking up Kettle's 720 yen unit cost by 50% gives 1,080 yen. The value stack says a home drinker would get 2,100 yen of value. The 1,020 yen between them (2,100 - 1,080) is value the buyer would have paid for and cost-plus never asked for. Kettle's real price, 1,800, captures 720 yen of that gap and still leaves the buyer 300 yen ahead.

Loss two: the spiral. Many firms mark up a full cost that includes overhead spread over expected volume. Say 3,600,000 yen a month of overhead is allocated to the tin and the variable cost is 720 yen. At 10,000 tins: 360 a tin, full cost 1,080, price 1,620. A rival launches and the forecast drops to 8,000: overhead becomes 450, full cost 1,170, price 1,755. The higher price loses more buyers; at 6,000 tins: 600, 1,320, price 1,980. The method answers falling demand by raising the price.

The other way round. Ramanujam and Tacke's Monetizing Innovation (Wiley 2016) is subtitled How Smart Companies Design the Product Around the Price: decide what buyers will pay before you build, then design a product and a cost that fit (publisher page read; the book's content is reported). Value first, cost second.

Why a price also tells buyers something about the product is Economics' territory: Economics, prices are information ↗. All numbers here are constructed Kettle & Co examples; the volumes are assumed, not estimated.

Self-studyWhen cost-plus is the right answer2 min▶

Regulated prices. Where a regulator sets the allowed return, as in many utilities, the price is cost plus an approved margin by design. The value conversation happens with the regulator, not the buyer.

Cost-reimbursed contracts. Some government and defence contracts pay audited cost plus a fee. The buyer has chosen to carry the cost risk, and the contract says how the price is built.

Commodities. When the product is genuinely undifferentiated, competition pushes the price toward cost anyway; the differentiation values in the stack are close to zero and the reference value is the market price.

Even in these cases, cost-plus is the floor check everywhere else: no value story justifies a price below unit cost for long. Use it as the floor, not the method.

Real world

The year Kettle sold out at 1,080. In its first year Kettle priced the tin at cost-plus, 1,080 yen, and sold out every harvest. The founder read sell-outs as success. The finance partner read them as a signal: buyers were queueing for something priced 1,020 yen under its value ceiling. Moving to 1,800 kept the subscription list growing and added 720 yen of contribution per tin. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Build-along

Build a value stack for one product

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

No code, one page. Pick one product and one segment of buyers, or use Kettle's 100 g tin for a home drinker. Five steps take you from "we are premium" to a ceiling with evidence behind every yen. Do step 1 together, steps 2 to 4 in pairs, then read your step 5 sentence aloud. Every differentiator needs a source: a survey, a tasting, a reorder rate, a lost-deal note. A yen value with no evidence is a guess and should be marked as one.

Step 1 · Name the next best alternative and its price. For one segment, ask what they would buy if your product disappeared tomorrow. Not your closest rival by your definition: the buyer's. Write its price for the same quantity.

★ Step 1 · reference valuesegment: ____ next best alternative: ____ price for the same quantity: ____ how we know (survey, interviews, switching data): ____ Kettle (constructed): home drinker · supermarket premium loose-leaf 100 g · 1,300 yen · 20 subscriber calls

Step 2 · List the positives, each with a yen value and its evidence. Only what this segment values and the alternative lacks. Put a yen value on each, then write where the number came from. No evidence? Write "guess" next to it.

★ Step 2 · positive differentiation+ ____ yen because ____ evidence: ____ + ____ yen because ____ evidence: ____ + ____ yen because ____ evidence: ____ Kettle: +600 freshness, harvest-dated single estate (blind tasting) · +400 subscription convenience (reorder data)

Step 3 · List the negatives. Where is the alternative better for this buyer? Waiting, minimums, switching effort, risk of the unfamiliar. Leaving these out is how value stacks get flattering.

★ Step 3 · negative differentiation- ____ yen because ____ evidence: ____ - ____ yen because ____ evidence: ____ Kettle: -200 wait for delivery and minimum order (cart drop-offs at the minimum)

Step 4 · Add up economic value. Reference value plus positives minus negatives. Show the sum so anyone can check it.

★ Step 4 · economic valuereference ____ + positives ____ - negatives ____ = economic value ____ Kettle: 1,300 + 600 + 400 - 200 = 2,100 yen

Step 5 · Place everything on one line and write the sentence. Unit cost, cost-plus price, competitor band, today's price, economic value, low to high. Then say where the price sits and why the buyer should switch. Read it aloud.

★ Step 5 · the line and the sentenceunit cost ____ · cost-plus ____ · rivals ____ to ____ · today ____ · economic value ____ "Our price of ___ sits ___ below the value of ___ for ___, which is their reason to switch from ___. Cost-plus would have charged ___." Kettle: 720 · 1,080 · 1,500 to 1,700 · 1,800 · 2,100 "Our price of 1,800 yen sits 300 below the 2,100 yen value for home drinkers, which is their reason to switch from supermarket loose-leaf. Cost-plus would have charged 1,080."

Keep the worksheet. The value stack sets the ceiling for the list price. Business session 3 follows the money the other way: from that list price down through every discount, rebate and allowance to what actually reaches the bank.

Homework

Before Business session 3

30 min
  1. Ask five customers what they would buy instead if your product disappeared. Write down the alternatives and their prices; count how many named a direct rival.
  2. Find out how your current price was set. Cost-plus, matched to a rival, or a value argument? Ask whoever set it, and write the method in one line.
  3. Mark every yen in your value stack as evidenced or guessed. For the biggest guess, name the cheapest way to get evidence for it.
  4. Build a second stack for a different segment of the same product. Compare the two ceilings and note which differentiators changed.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Business session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · In the economic value stack, what is the reference value?

Economic value starts from the next best alternative for that buyer, then adds positive and subtracts negative differentiation. For Kettle's home drinker: 1,300 + 600 + 400 - 200 = 2,100 yen.

2 · A firm marks up a full unit cost that includes overhead spread over expected volume. Demand falls. What does cost-plus do next?

Fewer units carry the same overhead, so cost per unit rises and the markup lifts the price. In the constructed Kettle loop: 10,000 tins gives 1,620 yen, 8,000 gives 1,755, 6,000 gives 1,980.

3 · Kettle's tin costs 720 yen and is worth 2,100 yen to a home drinker. Where should the price sit?

Cost is the floor and economic value the ceiling. At 2,100 a well-informed buyer is indifferent; at 1,800 they keep 300 yen of value, which is why they switch.
