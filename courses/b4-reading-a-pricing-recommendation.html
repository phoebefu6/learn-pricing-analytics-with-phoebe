Reading a pricing recommendation

Sooner or later a deck lands on your desk that says "raise all prices 8%", with one elasticity and one profit number. It may be right. Tonight gives you seven questions that tell you whether anyone can know, walks the eight years of regulator work on dynamic and personalised pricing so you can answer the "is this even allowed?" question with dates, and ends with a red pen on a constructed Kettle & Co deck.

Where we start

You now have the profit line and the discount break-even (Business session 1), three ways to set a price (session 2), and the leaks between list and pocket (session 3). The last skill is the one you use most: reading somebody else's recommendation. Most pricing decks are not wrong so much as unfinished: a borrowed number, a missing range, a profit figure with no base. Tonight is a checklist for finding the gaps, a dated map of what regulators have said about prices that move with demand or with who you are, and practice on a deck. Part 1 · covers the challenge checklist for a "raise prices 8%" deck

Seven questions for an 8% price rise

5 min live

A price recommendation rests on one borrowed number, the elasticity, and on a handful of choices nobody wrote down. Each question below either has an answer in the deck or becomes one request back to the author. A "no" is not a rejection; it is the next thing to ask for. Only when every answer is in does the move get decided.

LiveSeven questions for any pricing deck4 min▶

1 · Whose elasticity, estimated on what products? An elasticity belongs to the products and buyers it was measured on. A number estimated on a marketplace full of other sellers' goods is not your product's number, however carefully it was fitted. How such numbers are estimated, and how they go wrong, lives in Economics: Economics, the elasticity bench ↗.

2 · Over what range of prices? An elasticity describes how buyers responded to the price moves in the data. If the products in the data never moved more than a few percent, an 8% rise sits outside anything observed. Ask for the smallest and largest price change behind the number.

3 · A point or a range? One number with two decimals hides how unsure it is. Analyst session 3 hands Kettle a range of -1.2 to -2.4 and prints: "No price move wins across the whole range: every move depends on the point estimate." If the range straddles the move's break-even, no slide can tell you the answer: Analyst session 3, deciding with a borrowed elasticity.

4 · What horizon? A month's response is not a year's. Customers who keep buying next week may switch brands at the next stock-up, and customers who leave may come back. Ask whether the number describes the first weeks or a settled state.

5 · What do competitors do? An elasticity measured while rivals held still says nothing about a market where they follow you up, or hold and advertise the gap. Ask for the scenario the deck assumes.

6 · What margin base? "Profit +X%" means nothing until you know which profit. Business session 1 showed a 1% price move is worth 1 / operating margin of operating profit, but only m (the contribution margin) per unit: Business session 1.

7 · What past move was measured against a comparison group? The best evidence is your own: a previous price change or promotion, compared with products or periods that did not get it. Analyst session 6 runs exactly that ladder on real rows: Analyst session 6, the promo and discount lab.

Self-studyThe number that sounds precise3 min▶

"Elasticity -0.83" reads as more trustworthy than "somewhere between -0.5 and -1.5". It is not. Two decimals describe the arithmetic, not the evidence. Every estimate comes with a standard error; the honest form is a range, and the decision is whether the range sits wholly on one side of the move's break-even.

The break-even for Kettle's +8%. At a contribution margin m = 60%, a price rise of x = 8% keeps contribution flat when the elasticity equals ln(m / (m + x)) / ln(1 + x) = ln(0.60 / 0.68) / ln(1.08) = -0.1252 / 0.0770 = about -1.63. Less steep than -1.63 and the rise adds contribution; steeper and it loses. Constructed Kettle & Co example, the same formula Analyst session 3 uses.

So the first thing to do with a precise-looking point is to ask for its range, then put the break-even next to it. If the whole range is flatter than -1.63, the move pays across it. If the range crosses -1.63, the deck's confidence is borrowed from the decimals.

Real world

The benchmark that came from somewhere else. Kettle's agency deck quoted "elasticity -0.8, industry benchmark". Asked for the source, the agency found it came from a report on packaged beverages sold through supermarkets, not online loose-leaf tea, and covered price moves of a few percent. Two of the seven questions failed before anyone opened a spreadsheet. The commercial lead asked for the range and the products it was estimated on, and put the decision back a fortnight. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Part 2 · covers what regulators have found about dynamic and personalised pricing

What the FTC, CMA and OECD found, 2018 to 2026

5 min live

The second question a pricing deck now meets is "are we allowed to do this?". Nobody on this page can answer that for your market: this is not legal advice. What you can do is know what three regulators have published, with dates, and read each release for what it actually found. Everything below was read at source on ftc.gov, gov.uk and the OECD note; these are moving files, so re-verify before relying on it.

LiveEight years in one line4 min▶

The one line. Regulators have studied personalised pricing since 2018, found it can help and can harm, found the data being collected, and so far moved toward disclosure rather than a ban.

OECD, 20 November 2018. The background note "Personalised Pricing in the Digital Era" (DAF/COMP(2018)13) says it can improve allocative efficiency and serve low-end consumers who would otherwise be left out; in some cases it lowers total consumer welfare and feels unfair; and "it is still hard to find evidence of actual cases".

CMA, 19 January 2021. "Algorithms: How they can reduce competition and harm consumers": personalised pricing can be beneficial, raising output and consumer welfare, and can harm, especially when it is complex or opaque.

CMA, 5 April 2022. "Online Choice Architecture", discussion paper CMA155: a taxonomy of 21 practices, with drip pricing among those the literature finds almost always harmful, and a warning about opaque personalised pricing.

FTC, 23 July 2024. 6(b) orders to eight firms, by a 5-0 vote: Mastercard, Revionics, Bloomreach, JPMorgan Chase, Task Software, PROS, Accenture and McKinsey & Co.

FTC, 17 January 2025. Preliminary staff findings from six of those firms' documents, by a 3-2 vote: the data used can include precise location or browser history, and behaviour down to mouse movements. The study "is still ongoing".

CMA, 20 June 2025. "Dynamic pricing: project update": dynamic pricing can be consistent with effective competition and good outcomes; consumer law does not generally prohibit pricing strategies; the risk is prices changing rapidly while consumers are confused.

CMA, 25 September 2025. Ticketmaster and Oasis case closed (the next card).

FTC, 19 August 2026. A proposed enforcement policy statement on personalised pricing, by a 2-0 vote: the FTC says it does not have the legal authority to ban personalised pricing in all circumstances, and that undisclosed use of personal data to set prices could violate the FTC Act.

FTC, 28 October 2026. A new 6(b) inquiry into personalised pricing and compensation, with orders to nine firms including Delta, Uber, Walmart and Target, by a 2-0 vote.

Self-studyTicketmaster: the dynamic pricing that wasn't3 min▶

The Oasis reunion ticket sale became the headline example of "dynamic pricing" in 2024 and 2025. When the CMA closed the case on 25 September 2025, it said it had not found evidence of real-time algorithmic pricing. The issue was simpler: standing tickets were sold in two tiers at two prices without fans being told.

Ticketmaster gave undertakings, among them 24 hours' notice before tiered pricing is used. The CMA made no finding that consumer law was infringed.

The reading lesson. The press called it an algorithm; the regulator found a disclosure problem. Before you cite a case as evidence for or against dynamic pricing, read what the closing statement actually found. It is often narrower, and more useful, than the headline.

Real world

A 3-2 vote that was not about pricing. The FTC's January 2025 preliminary findings passed 3-2. Read quickly, that looks like a split on surveillance pricing itself. Read at source, Commissioner Ferguson, joined by Commissioner Holyoak, dissented from releasing summaries before the study was finished: a process objection, not a defence of the practice. When a deck cites a split vote, ask what the split was about.

Part 3 · covers the help-or-harm disagreement and the shared middle

Can help, can harm, and where they agree

5 min live

Read together, the releases do not line up into a verdict. The OECD and the CMA each say personalised pricing can help some buyers and can harm others; FTC staff document what data is being used. Nobody here resolves that, and you should not either. What you can use is the middle they share: both lean toward telling customers rather than banning the practice, and both admit the evidence of real cases is thin.

LiveWhere they agree3 min▶

The help side. The OECD note says personalised pricing can improve allocative efficiency and reach low-end buyers a single price would leave out. The CMA's 2021 paper says it can raise output and consumer welfare, and its 2025 update says dynamic pricing can be consistent with effective competition.

The harm side. The same OECD note says it can lower total consumer welfare and create a perception of unfairness. The CMA warns about practices that are complex or opaque. FTC staff found the inputs can include precise location, browser history and mouse movements.

The shared middle. Both regulators converge on disclosure rather than a ban: the CMA's Ticketmaster undertakings are about telling fans before tiers are used, and the FTC's 2026 proposed statement says it cannot ban the practice in all circumstances but that undisclosed use of personal data could break the law. And both admit the evidence is thin: the OECD's "hard to find evidence of actual cases", the CMA's Oasis finding of no real-time algorithm.

What that means for a deck. A proposal that only works if customers do not find out is the proposal to send back. A proposal you would happily explain on the product page is on the side every regulator above can live with. That is a practical reading, not a legal opinion.

Self-studyDynamic is not personalised2 min▶

Dynamic pricing changes the price with demand, for everyone at once: the same tin costs more on a busy day and less on a quiet one, whoever is looking. Personalised pricing changes the price with who you are: two customers looking at the same tin at the same moment see different prices.

The regulators treat them differently. The CMA's 2025 update is about dynamic pricing and says it can sit with effective competition; the FTC's surveillance pricing study and 2026 statement are about personal data setting individual prices. Decks blur the two all the time ("our dynamic pricing engine uses customer signals"). Ask which one it is, because the risk and the disclosure question are different.

Price discrimination theory (why selling the same thing at different prices can raise or lower welfare) lives in Economics: Economics, pricing with the number ↗.

Real world

The returning-customer tin. A growth analyst at Kettle proposed showing returning customers a higher price on the 100 g tin, on the theory that loyal buyers would not notice. The team asked three questions: would we put this on the product page? (no); is it dynamic or personalised? (personalised, keyed on purchase history); is there a measured comparison? (none). The answer was to drop it and test a published loyalty price instead: one price, shown to everyone, with a visible member discount. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Build-along

Red-pen the deck

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

One slide, five steps, one pen. The slide below is Kettle's constructed agency recommendation: "Raise all prices 8%: elasticity -0.8 (industry benchmark), +6.4% profit". For each step, write the checklist question, what the deck answers, and what you would ask for. Do step 1 together, steps 2 to 4 in pairs, and read your step 5 sentence aloud. Constructed Kettle & Co example throughout.

Step 1 · Whose elasticity, on what products and prices? Questions 1 and 2 of the checklist. Find the source line and ask what it was estimated on.

★ Step 1 · whose numberQuestion: whose elasticity, estimated on which products, over what price moves? Deck says: "-0.8 (industry benchmark)". No products, no price range. Ask for: the report, the products and channel it covers, and the largest price change in its data. Is +8% inside that range?

Step 2 · Point or range? Question 3. Put the break-even next to whatever range you get back.

★ Step 2 · the rangeQuestion: is -0.8 a point or a range? Deck says: a point, carried into a precise -6.4%. Break-even for +8% at m = 60%: ln(0.60 / 0.68) / ln(1.08) = about -1.63 Ask for: the range. If it straddles -1.63, the move "depends on the point estimate". Constructed: if the true number were -1.854 (Economics' estimate on Olist marketplace rows, a different product set), contribution would change by 1.08^-1.854 x 1,224 / 1,080 - 1 = about -1.7%: the rise would lose.

Step 3 · Horizon and competitors. Questions 4 and 5. The footnote is silent on both, which is itself the answer.

★ Step 3 · horizon and rivalsQuestion: over what horizon, and what do competitors do? Deck says: "horizon, competitors not stated". Ask for: is -0.8 a first-month or a settled response? Which rivals sell loose-leaf tea online, and does the plan assume they hold, follow, or advertise the gap?

Step 4 · Which margin base, and check the arithmetic. Question 6. The slide's "+6.4%" is the volume fall from its own footnote, relabelled as profit.

★ Step 4 · the profit lineQuestion: which profit, on which margin base? Deck says: "Profit +6.4%", and 0.8 x 8% = 6.4% is the VOLUME change. Redo it at -0.8, constant elasticity, tin at 1,800 yen, cost 720 yen: volume 1.08^-0.8 = 0.940 (-6.0%) per tin 1,944 - 720 = 1,224 yen, vs 1,080 (x 1.133) contribution 1.133 x 0.940 = 1.066 (+6.6% of contribution) per 100 yen of old revenue: contribution 60 -> 63.9, fixed 50 held, operating profit 10 -> 13.9 (+39%), if every product shared these numbers. Ask for: the margin base, named. +6.6% and +39% are the same yen.

Step 5 · Measured evidence, then the sentence. Question 7, then write what you would do and read it aloud.

★ Step 5 · the sentenceQuestion: what past price move was measured against a comparison group? Deck says: nothing. Ask for: Kettle's last price change or promotion, compared with products or weeks that did not get it. "The deck's -0.8 is a borrowed point with no range; break-even is -1.63. If the true number is flatter, +8% adds about 6.6% of contribution; if it is steeper, it loses. We will raise the tin 8% on one channel for eight weeks against a held channel before any company-wide move."

Keep the red pen. The five marks work on any recommendation, including your own. Homework

Before you start the Analyst track

30 min
  1. Red-pen one real deck. Take the last pricing recommendation you saw and mark it against the seven questions. Count the "no"s.
  2. Compute one break-even elasticity. For a price move on your table, with your contribution margin m and move x: ln(m / (m + x)) / ln(1 + x). Put it next to the elasticity the deck used.
  3. Name your pricing as dynamic, personalised, or neither. For each price that varies, write one line: does it change with demand for everyone, or with who the customer is?
  4. Re-verify one regulator release that matters in your market, at source, and note its date. Bring it, with the date you read it.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Business session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A deck quotes "elasticity -0.8". What is the first question to ask?

An elasticity belongs to the products and price moves it was measured on. A benchmark from someone else's products, or from moves far smaller than yours, may not be your number at all.

2 · What did the FTC's proposed policy statement of 19 August 2026 say about personalised pricing?

The statement is about disclosure, not a ban. It is proposed and recent: re-verify before relying on it.

3 · Where do the OECD, CMA and FTC material agree?

They disagree on the effect (it can help and can harm) and converge on the remedy: tell customers. The OECD says real cases are hard to find; the CMA found no real-time algorithm in the Oasis case.
