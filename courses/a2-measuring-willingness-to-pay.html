Measuring willingness to pay

Before a new tin has a till history, the only way to ask what it is worth is to ask people. Two survey methods do most of that work: Van Westendorp's four price questions, which give a range, and Gabor-Granger's yes-or-no ladder, which gives a rough demand curve. Tonight reads both on the same 200 answers, shows why one survey can honestly report two acceptable ranges, and why the price that maximises revenue is not the one that maximises profit. Kettle & Co's survey answers are constructed and seeded, so every learner sees the same numbers.

Part 1 · covers Van Westendorp's price sensitivity meter

Four questions, four cumulative curves

5 min live

Van Westendorp's price sensitivity meter, presented at the ESOMAR Congress in 1976 (reported), asks each respondent for four prices rather than one. Count, at every price, the share of people whose answer sits on the wrong side of it, and you get four cumulative curves: two that fall as price rises, two that rise. Where they cross is the whole output of the method.

LiveFour questions, four curves3 min▶

The questions, paraphrased. At what price would this tin be so expensive you would not consider buying it (too expensive)? So cheap you would doubt its quality (too cheap)? Starting to get expensive, though you might still buy (expensive)? A bargain, good value for the money (cheap)? The CRAN pricesensitivitymeter manual describes the method in these four parts.

The curves. At each price, count the share whose "too cheap" answer is at or above it: that curve falls as price rises, because fewer people think a higher price is suspiciously low. "Cheap" falls the same way. "Expensive" and "too expensive" count the share whose answer is at or below the price, so they rise.

The two crossings every source agrees on. Optimal price point = too cheap x too expensive: the price where the fewest people reject it from either end. Indifference price point = cheap x expensive: the price where as many call it a bargain as call it getting expensive. For Kettle's tin the widget prints 1,600 yen and 1,624 yen.

Self-studyMultiple crossings need a rule2 min▶

Cumulative curves from real answers are stepped and noisy, and two of them can touch, part and cross again. The CRAN manual notes that multiple crossings are possible, so any report needs a stated rule for which one counts. The widget here scans up from 500 yen in 50-yen steps and takes the first crossing, interpolating between the two steps on either side of it. Whatever rule you choose, write it beside the number, and when a second crossing sits far from the first, show both rather than hide one: it usually means two groups of buyers with different ideas of the product.

Real world

The tin that had no history. Kettle planned a new 100 g tin of a single-estate tea and had no sales to read a price from. The team ran the four questions on 200 tea drinkers before choosing a launch price. The optimal price point came out at 1,600 yen and the indifference point at 1,624 yen, close together, which said the answers were not split into two camps. Constructed Kettle & Co example; the widget below prints both numbers.

Part 2 · covers the two published definitions of the acceptable range

One survey, two acceptable ranges

5 min live

The bounds of the acceptable range are where the sources part ways. The original definition, as the CRAN manual gives it, crosses each "too" curve with the opposite of its neighbour: too cheap x not cheap, too expensive x not expensive. A narrower definition used by some firms, and given on Wikipedia, crosses too cheap with expensive and too expensive with cheap. Same 200 answers, two ranges. Neither is a mistake; they answer slightly different questions, and the report has to say which one it used.

LiveWhich range do you report?3 min▶

Say which definition. "Acceptable range 1,117 to 2,330 yen" and "acceptable range 1,360 to 1,900 yen" are both correct readings of Kettle's survey. A range reported without its definition cannot be checked or compared with last year's study, which may have used the other one.

Report both when a decision rides on a bound. If the proposal is to price the tin at 2,000 yen, it sits inside the original range and outside the narrower one. That is not a detail for a footnote: show both ranges, say the price is acceptable under one reading and not the other, and let the decision-maker see the disagreement.

Lean on the points both agree on. The optimal price point and the indifference price point do not depend on the definition. When the ranges disagree, those two are the safer anchors.

Self-studyNewton, Miller and Smith's extension2 min▶

A 1993 extension by Newton, Miller and Smith is reported to add purchase-intent questions after the four price questions: at the respondent's own "cheap" and "expensive" prices, how likely would they be to buy? That turns a range of acceptable prices into an estimate of how many would buy at each, closer to a demand curve. The details here are reported rather than read in the paper; check the original before building a survey around it. The questionnaire itself, wording, order and sample, belongs to a survey design course.

Real world

Two slides, one survey. Kettle's brand team presented "anything up to 2,330 yen is acceptable"; finance, reading the same file with the other definition, said "above 1,900 yen we lose people". Both were right about their definition, and neither slide said which one it used. The fix was one line on every range: the definition, and both ranges whenever a proposed price sat between 1,900 and 2,330 yen. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Part 3 · covers Gabor-Granger and stated versus revealed willingness to pay

The yes-or-no ladder, and its two peaks

5 min live

Gabor and Granger's method, published in the Journal of Advertising Research in 1964 (method reported), asks a simpler question: at this price, would you buy, yes or no? Ask it at a set of prices and the share saying yes forms a stepwise demand curve. Multiply each step by its price and you have revenue; subtract the unit cost first and you have contribution. The two peaks are not at the same price.

LiveRevenue peak is not the profit peak3 min▶

The ladder. Each respondent says yes or no at 1,200, 1,400, 1,600, 1,800, 2,000 and 2,200 yen. The share saying yes falls from 95.5% to 36.0%: that is the stepwise demand curve, and it is the whole of the method's raw output.

Two products of the same curve. Revenue per 200 asked is price x buyers: 1,600 x 157 = 251,200 yen, the highest in the table. Contribution subtracts the unit cost first: (1,800 - 640) x 130 = 150,800 yen, just above (1,600 - 640) x 157 = 150,720 yen. A higher price loses buyers, but each remaining buyer carries more margin, so the contribution peak sits at or above the revenue peak whenever cost is positive.

Read the size of the gap, not only its direction. 150,800 against 150,720 yen is a difference of 80 yen per 200 asked: the contribution curve is almost flat between 1,600 and 1,800 yen. With six prices 200 yen apart and stated answers, that flat top says "somewhere in this window", not "exactly 1,800".

Self-studyConjoint, in one paragraph2 min▶

Both methods tonight ask about price alone, with the product held fixed. Conjoint analysis asks respondents to choose between whole products that differ on several features at once, price among them, and infers how much each feature is worth in money from the trade-offs they make. It is the tool for "what is a larger tin, or a single-estate origin, worth to the buyer?" and it needs careful design and a choice model to analyse. It is named here so you know where to go next; it is not taught on this page, and its design belongs with a survey design course.

Real world

From the survey window to the till. Kettle's team did not launch at the revenue peak. People say yes more readily in a survey than at a till, where the money is real, so the team treated 1,600 to 1,800 yen as the window to test rather than the answer, launched the tin inside it, and set a date to compare the till against the survey. Stated willingness to pay is a range finder; revealed behaviour is the check. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Stated against revealed. Both methods record what people say, not what they pay. Treat their output as a range to test, and take the test itself to Experimentation's A/B test session; once there is till history, Analyst session 3 decides with an elasticity estimated from it.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which two Van Westendorp curves cross at the optimal price point?

The optimal price point is where the fewest people reject the price from either end: too cheap x too expensive. Cheap x expensive is the indifference price point (1,624 yen for Kettle); too cheap x not cheap is a bound under the original definition.

2 · Why can one Van Westendorp survey report two different acceptable ranges?

The answers and the counts are identical; only the crossings read as bounds differ. That is why Kettle's survey gives 1,117 to 2,330 yen or 1,360 to 1,900 yen, while the optimal price point stays at 1,600 under both.

3 · In Kettle's Gabor-Granger table, revenue peaks at 1,600 yen and contribution at 1,800 yen. Why are they different?

(1,800 - 640) x 130 = 150,800 yen beats (1,600 - 640) x 157 = 150,720 yen. Both columns come from the same stated answers; the gap is tiny, so read it as a window rather than a point.
