Promo lift and the dip after

A promotion report says the discount added so many units. That number is actual sales minus a baseline, the sales you would have had without the discount, and nobody ever observes the baseline. Choose it badly and the season, the trend or next month's sales get credited to the price cut. Tonight works three baselines on real Olist marketplace rows around Black Friday 2017, looks at the month after for a dip, and asks what a promoted product took from its siblings.

Part 0

Where we start

Analyst session 3 decided with a number somebody else measured. Tonight you measure one. The rows are real: real Olist marketplace rows (Brazilian e-commerce, delivered orders, prices in BRL), monthly units and mean prices for every product sold in October, November and December 2017. Some sellers cut their November price and some did not. That split is the only reason a fair lift can be computed at all, and it comes with a condition: sellers chose which products to cut, so the comparison rests on an assumption you will learn to say out loud.

Part 1 · covers baseline and lift

Lift is actual minus baseline, and the baseline is the argument

5 min live

Actual sales are on the report. The baseline is a claim about a month that did not happen. Last month as the baseline counts the season as lift. The same month last year counts a year of growth as lift. A comparison group in the same month, products that did not get the discount, takes the season out, but only if those products would have moved in step with the promoted ones. On the real rows the choice changes the answer by more than half.

LiveThree baselines, three lifts3 min▶

Last month. October as the baseline for November. The cut group went from 310 to 654 units: 344 extra units, +111.0%. That number counts Black Friday, the run-up to Christmas and everything else November brings. Across the whole panel, products that cut and products that did not, November units rose +74.7% on October. Most of the cut group's jump would have happened anyway.

The same month last year. November 2016 as the baseline for November 2017. It takes the season out, because both rows are Novembers, and puts a year of growth in: a marketplace that grew through 2017 shows lift from growth alone. The rows here start in July 2017, so this baseline cannot be computed on them. On your own data, scale last year's month by the year's trend before you subtract, and say that you did.

A comparison group in the same month. The 54 products that held price grew +65.5% from October to November. Applied to the cut group's October, that growth sets an expected November of 513 units. 654 minus 513 leaves 141 extra units, out of the 344 that last month credited. The comparison keeps the season out of the answer as long as the held products would have tracked the cut ones without the discount.

★ The arithmetic, on real rowslast month: 654 - 310 = 344 extra units (+111.0%) comparison group: 310 x (513 / 310) = 513 expected 654 - 513 = 141 extra units share of the naive figure the comparison keeps: 141 / 344 = 41%
Self-studyParallel trends, in one paragraph2 min▶

The comparison group works only if, without any discount, the cut products would have grown at the held products' rate. That is the parallel-trends assumption, and on these rows it is an assumption, not a fact: sellers chose which products to cut. A seller who cut price on the products they expected to sell best at Black Friday would make the cut group look better than the discount deserves; a seller who cut price on slow movers would do the opposite. Look at the months before: if the two groups moved together from July to October, the assumption is easier to believe, never proven. The method and its checks are taught in Experimentation, difference-in-differences ↗; when you cannot assign the discount yourself, read Experimentation, when you can't randomize ↗.

Real world

Black Friday sits inside the November row. Brazil's Black Friday 2017 fell on Friday 24 November; the Ebit report, via Mercado e Consumo on 29 November 2017, called it the main offers day. Every product on the marketplace felt it, whether or not its seller cut the price. That is why the whole panel rose +74.7% in November, and why a promotion report that uses October as the baseline hands Black Friday to the discount.

Part 2 · covers pull-forward and carryover

The month after: a dip, or a tail

5 min live

A discount can create new purchases, or it can move purchases that would have happened anyway to an earlier date. If it only moves them, the month after sits below its baseline: customers bought in November what they would have bought in December. That is pull-forward, and the dip is how you see it. The opposite also happens: new buyers come back, and the months after sit above baseline. That is carryover. Either way, the promotion month alone is not the lift.

LiveNet lift = promo month + the months after3 min▶

The rule. Net lift is the promotion month's extra units plus the extra units, positive or negative, of every month after, each against the same kind of baseline. A dip subtracts; a tail adds. Stop counting when the gap is back to zero, and say how many months you looked at.

All products, real rows. In December the cut group sold 388 units against an expected 365 from the held group's growth: +23 units, not a dip. Add it to November's 141 and the two-month net lift is 164 extra units. At monthly grain, for all products, there is no sign of pull-forward.

One category, real rows. Narrow the scope to Home and garden in the lab of Analyst session 6 and the month after turns negative. The lab prints: "Rung 4: December -16.6 units against the held group, so 30.5 extra units over November and December." Same method, a different slice, and the answer changes sign. Report the scope with the number.

★ Net lift over two months, all productsNovember: 654 - 513 = +141 December: 388 - 365 = +23 net: 141 + 23 = 164 extra units
Self-studyStockpiling and why daily data matters2 min▶

Products that keep, like tea, detergent or batteries, invite stockpiling: a buyer who would have bought one a month buys three at the discount and nothing for two months. Products that spoil, or that buyers cannot store, have less room for it. That is where to look hardest for a dip.

The grain decides what you can see. These rows are monthly, so Black Friday is one day inside the November row and December is one number. A dip in December's first week that recovers by Christmas would vanish inside the month total; the rows cannot tell you whether that happened. Daily or weekly data, with the same comparison group, would show Black Friday itself and the days after it. When the dip matters to the decision, ask for the finer grain before you declare there was none.

Real world

The dip that depended on the slice. On the real Olist rows, all products together show December above the comparison: +23 units. The 37 Home and garden products, 16 cut and 20 held, show December below it: -16.6 units, leaving 30.5 extra units over the two months. Neither answer is wrong. A promotion review that reports only the total would never see that one category gave back part of its November.

Part 3 · covers cannibalisation and price test design

The siblings pay for it, and the test that would tell you

5 min live

A promoted product can take its extra units from a sibling: the same buyer, the same occasion, a cheaper pack this month. The promoted product's lift then counts sales the category already had. Measuring it needs sales of the siblings beside the promoted product, and the Olist panel does not carry that link: products are anonymised, with no tie between a product and its siblings. So cannibalisation is taught here on a constructed Kettle example, and on the real rows it stays unmeasured.

LiveLift for the category, not the product3 min▶

The question. The decision was "should we discount the tin?", and the business lives on the category, not the tin. So the lift that answers it is the category's: promoted product plus every sibling a buyer could have switched from, each against its own baseline.

Kettle, constructed. In the promotion month the 100 g tin sold 2,400 against a baseline of 2,000: +400 units, +20.0%. The 250 g caddy sold 750 against 900: -150 units. Category net: +250 units on a baseline of 2,900, +8.6%. Count in tea and it nearly disappears: the tin added 40 kg, the caddy lost 37.5 kg, so the category sold 2.5 kg more tea, at a discount on the tin.

★ Category lift · Constructed Kettle & Co exampletin: 2,400 - 2,000 = +400 units (400 / 2,000 = +20.0%) caddy: 750 - 900 = -150 units category: 3,150 - 2,900 = +250 units (250 / 2,900 = +8.6%) in tea: 400 x 100 g = 40 kg; 150 x 250 g = 37.5 kg; net +2.5 kg

On the real rows. The Olist panel cannot do this. Products are anonymised and nothing links a product to its siblings, so a cut product's 141 extra units may include sales taken from a similar product in the held group. If it did, the held group's baseline is a little low and the gap a little high. Say that in the report as a limit, not a footnote.

Self-studyWhat a clean price test needs2 min▶

Everything above reads a promotion after the fact, with a comparison group sellers chose. A test you design removes that weak point. Three things make it clean. Randomise who gets the price: by store, region, customer or week, decided by a random draw, so the cut group cannot be the products or customers someone expected to do well. Hold a group back: a share that keeps the old price through the test and the weeks after, so you can see both the lift and the dip against the same baseline. Size it in advance: decide how many units or customers you need to see the lift you care about before you start, and do not stop early because the number looks good.

The sizing and the analysis belong to Experimentation, run an A/B test ↗; use its method rather than a rule of thumb. For price tests, add two pricing-specific checks: measure the siblings as well as the tested product, and run long enough to cover the month after.

Real world

Kettle's November tin promotion. Kettle's online shop ran 10% off the 100 g tin for November. The campaign recap compared November with October and credited the whole rise to the discount. The analyst's rebuild changed three things: the baseline became the same month for tins outside the promotion, which took the season out and left the tin at +400 units, +20.0%; the caddy went into the count, which took out the 150 units that switched and left the category at +250, +8.6%; and December went in, to look for buyers who had stocked up. The decision on next November was taken on the category's number. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Homework

Before Analyst session 5

30 min
  1. Find a past promotion report in your own business and write down the baseline it used. If it does not say, that is your first finding.
  2. Recompute its lift against a comparison group: products, stores or regions that did not get the discount in the same weeks. Note how much of the reported lift survives.
  3. Look at the month after against the same comparison. Write the net lift over the promotion and the following month, and the grain you had.
  4. List the siblings a buyer could have switched from, and check whether their sales fell during the promotion. If you cannot get their rows, write that down as the report's limit.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why is last month a bad baseline for a November promotion?

On the real rows the cut group's last-month lift was 344 extra units; against products that held price in the same month, 141. The difference is Black Friday and the run-up to Christmas, which every product felt.

2 · In a different scope, December comes in below the comparison group's expectation. What would that mean?

A dip after the promotion is the sign of pull-forward. In the Home and garden scope December came in at -16.6 units, leaving 30.5 extra units over the two months. For all products December was +23, no dip at monthly grain.

3 · Why can the Olist panel not show cannibalisation?

Cannibalisation is measured on siblings: the products a buyer would otherwise have bought. Without sibling links the panel can only report the promoted products' lift, which is why the limit belongs in the report.
