Learn Pricing Analytics with Phoebe · Business session 1 of 4

Price is the biggest lever

A price change is the one move that lands on profit whole: no extra tea to brew, no extra box to ship. That is why a small price slip hurts so much and why a discount has to buy back a surprising amount of volume. Tonight puts the profit line on one page, checks the famous "1% of price is 11% of profit" claim against its own source, and works out what a discount must deliver before it pays. Kettle & Co, a direct-to-consumer tea brand, is constructed; the Black Friday rows at the end are real.

Part 1 · covers the profit line and the four levers

A price change lands on profit whole

5 min live

Profit is what is left of revenue after variable costs (the tea, the tin, the shipping) and fixed costs (the team, the rent). Move volume and costs move with it. Move price and nothing else moves: every extra yen goes straight to the thin layer at the top. That asymmetry is the whole reason pricing deserves its own analytics.

LiveThe four levers, worked on Kettle3 min▶

The line. For every 100 yen Kettle takes, 40 go on variable costs (tea, tin, packing, delivery), 50 on fixed costs (people, warehouse, software), and 10 is operating profit. Constructed, but a shape many consumer brands will recognise.

Price +1%, volume held. Revenue becomes 101 and no cost moves, so profit goes from 10 to 11: up 10%.

Volume +1%. Revenue rises by 1 but variable costs rise by 0.4, so profit gains 0.6: up 6%.

Costs down 1%. Variable costs 40 to 39.6 adds 0.4 (4%); fixed costs 50 to 49.5 adds 0.5 (5%).

The ranking is arithmetic, not a law of nature: it depends on the shape of your own line. That is the point of Part 2.

Self-studyWhy "volume held" is doing the heavy lifting2 min▶

Every lever above assumes the others stay still. Price is the one lever where that assumption is shakiest: raise the price and some customers buy less or leave. How many is the price elasticity. Here it is an input, never estimated; Economics estimates it on real Olist rows and shows why the naive number lies: Economics, why the naive number lies ↗. Analyst session 3 takes the number it produces, as a range, and asks which price move survives the whole range.

Real world

The 2% that funded a hire. Kettle's finance partner asked the commercial lead what a 2% list price rise on the 100 g tin would do if no customer noticed. On a 10% operating margin the answer is a 20% rise in operating profit, roughly the cost of the analyst they wanted to hire. The question that followed was the right one: how many customers would notice, and who has a number for that? Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Part 2 · covers the 1% claim and its margin base

"1% of price is 11% of profit", checked at source

5 min live

The most quoted number in pricing comes from Marn and Rosiello's 1992 Harvard Business Review article: for the average of 2,463 companies, a 1% price improvement, with no loss of volume, raised operating profit by 11.1%. It is true, and it is arithmetic. The same authors printed 8% in 2003 and 14% for a case company, because the answer is one divided by the operating margin.

LiveWhat the 1992 article actually says3 min▶

The exhibit. Marn and Rosiello compared four levers on the average economics of 2,463 companies in the Compustat database: a 1% improvement in price raised operating profit 11.1%, variable cost 7.8%, volume 3.3%, fixed cost 2.3%. Same ranking as Kettle's line, different sizes.

The condition. The text says it plainly: the 11.1% assumes no loss of volume. The same exhibit read backwards is the warning: a 1% price slip destroys 11.1% of operating profit.

The margin. The article does not print the operating margin behind 11.1%. Divide 1% by 11.1% and you get about 9%: that is our arithmetic, not their claim. Their own Castle Battery case runs at about 7% return on sales and gets 14%; the 2003 McKinsey update uses an S&P 1500 average with a 12.5% margin and gets 8%.

How to quote it. "On an average company with about a 9% operating margin, 1% of price was worth about 11% of operating profit, if volume held." Then say your own margin and your own number.

Self-studyIs the claim contested?2 min▶

No peer-reviewed critique of the figure turned up in the research for this session; the critique lives in the sources themselves. The arithmetic is not in dispute. What is disputed, deal by deal, is the condition: whether volume holds. Retellings often drop the margin and the condition and keep the 11, sometimes attributing it to other studies. The verifiable origin is the 1992 exhibit. When someone quotes the number at you, ask two questions: whose margin, and what happens to volume?

Real world

Three slides, three numbers, one source. A pricing vendor's slide said 11%, a consultant's said 8%, a textbook case said 14%. All three trace to Marn and colleagues, and all three are right for their margin. Kettle's 10% operating margin gives 10%. The useful slide is the one that shows the margin it used.

Part 3 · covers the discount break-even identity

What a discount has to buy back

5 min live

A discount runs the lever in reverse. Cut the price by d and every unit earns less: the contribution margin m (price minus variable cost, as a share of price) shrinks to m - d. To earn the same contribution you need more units, and the rise needed is d / (m - d). On a thin margin the number explodes.

LiveThe identity in three lines3 min▶

Before. Kettle's 100 g tin sells at 1,800 yen and costs 720 yen to make and ship: contribution 1,080 yen, margin m = 60%.

After a 10% discount. The tin sells at 1,620 yen and still costs 720: contribution 900 yen. Each tin now earns 900 / 1,080 = 83.3% of what it did.

Break-even. To earn the same total, sell 1,080 / 900 = 1.2 times as many tins: 20% more. In general, d / (m - d) = 0.10 / 0.50 = 20%. The McKinsey 2003 article prints the same arithmetic for its average company: a 5% cut needs 18.7% more volume just to offset it.

Break-even is the floor, not the goal: it says the discount gave nothing back, it does not say it was worth doing.

Self-studyWhich margin goes in the formula2 min▶

Use contribution margin (price minus the costs that move with each unit), not gross margin from the accounts and not operating margin. Operating margin answers Part 2's question (what a price move does to the bottom line); contribution margin answers this one (how many more units a cut needs). If shipping is free above a basket size, or a promotion brings a payment fee, those belong in the unit cost for the period of the promotion. The bigger the share of cost that is truly fixed, the higher m and the gentler the curve.

Real world

Black Friday 2017, measured. On real Olist rows, 29 products cut their November price more than 3% against October. Compared with the 54 that held price, and counting December, the promo lab in Analyst session 6 prints: "Rung 5: discount depth 7.9% at an assumed 60.0% cost ratio needs 24.6% more volume; measured 20.4%. It did not pay: -R$ 1,058.10." Olist publishes no costs, so the 60% is an assumption you can move; at 50% the same rows say it paid. Same identity, real rows.

Build-along

The discount worksheet

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

No code, one page. Use your own product, or Kettle's tin (1,800 yen, 720 yen unit cost, 10% operating margin across the business). Five steps take a discount proposal from "it will drive volume" to a number someone can check. Do step 1 together, steps 2 to 4 in pairs, then read your step 5 sentence aloud.

Step 1 · Write your profit line. Per 100 of revenue: variable costs, fixed costs, operating profit. Last year's accounts are close enough; round to whole numbers.

★ Step 1 · the lineper 100 of revenue: variable costs ____ fixed costs ____ operating profit ____ Kettle (constructed): variable 40 · fixed 50 · profit 10

Step 2 · Price the four levers. For a 1% move on each, write the change in profit and divide by profit. Rank them. If price is not first, your margin is unusually fat; say so.

★ Step 2 · the leversprice +1%: profit +____ = ____% volume +1%: profit +____ = ____% (revenue +1, variable costs +__) variable cost -1%: profit +____ = ____% fixed cost -1%: profit +____ = ____% Kettle: +10% · +6% · +4% · +5%

Step 3 · Break-even for the discount on the table. Take the product's contribution margin m (price minus unit cost, over price) and the proposed discount d. Volume rise needed = d / (m - d).

★ Step 3 · break-evenprice ____ unit cost ____ m = (price - cost) / price = ____ discount d = ____ volume rise needed = d / (m - d) = ____ Kettle tin: 1,800 yen, 720 yen, m = 60%; d = 15%: 0.15 / 0.45 = +33.3%

Step 4 · Write down the lift anyone has measured. Not the plan, the measurement: a past promotion compared with products or weeks that did not get it. If there is none, write "none" and the plan's number next to it.

★ Step 4 · evidencemeasured lift from a comparable past promotion: ____ (source: ____) planned lift in the proposal: ____ Kettle: none measured; plan says +25%

Step 5 · One sentence. Put break-even and evidence side by side and say what you would do. Read it aloud.

★ Step 5 · the sentence"A ___% discount needs ___% more volume to pay. The best evidence we have is ___. So we will ___." Kettle: "A 15% discount needs 33% more volume. The plan expects 25% and nobody has measured it. So we test it on one channel for four weeks against a holdout before any rollout."

Keep the worksheet. Business session 3 adds a line between price and profit that this sheet has assumed away: the money that leaves after the invoice. A discount that looks like 10% on the price list is often more by the time it reaches the bank.

Homework

Before Business session 2

30 min
  1. Find your operating margin in last year's accounts and compute 1 / margin. That is your version of the 11%.
  2. Find one product's contribution margin. Ask finance for the unit cost, including shipping and payment fees, and compute m.
  3. List the discounts running today and the depth of each. For the biggest, compute d / (m - d).
  4. Ask who measured the last promotion, and how. Bring the answer, even if it is "nobody".
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Business session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Kettle's operating margin is 10%. A 1% price rise with no change in volume raises operating profit by how much?

The gain is 1 / operating margin. 11.1% belongs to a company with about a 9% margin; Kettle's 10% gives 10%.

2 · What does Marn and Rosiello's 1992 article say about its 11.1%?

It is arithmetic on an average income statement, with volume held. The same authors printed 8% (2003) and 14% (Castle Battery) for other margins.

3 · A 10% discount on a product with a 40% contribution margin needs how much more volume just to break even?

Each unit now earns 30 instead of 40, so you need 40 / 30 = 1.333 times the units. Thinner margins make the curve steeper.
