Deciding with a borrowed elasticity

Someone else estimated the elasticity. Your job is to decide with it, and to know when you cannot. Tonight takes the number as an input that arrives as a range, turns each price move into a profit change with constant-elasticity arithmetic, finds the elasticity at which each move stops paying, and asks the one question that matters: is there a decision that holds wherever in the range the truth sits? Nothing on this page is estimated or refitted. Kettle & Co's 100 g tin is constructed.

Part 0

Where we start

Analyst session 2 asked people what they would pay. Tonight starts from a different input: an elasticity, handed over by whoever estimated it from sales data. Pricing teams rarely estimate it themselves, and they should not quietly re-estimate it to get a friendlier number. What they own is the arithmetic that turns it into a decision, and the discipline to notice when the uncertainty in the number is wider than the margin for error in the decision.

🎫 Clear all three quiz questions to stamp your passport Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class ★ Build-along = everyone builds it

★ What you walk out with tonight. Two formulas you can type into a spreadsheet: units after a price move under constant elasticity, and the break-even elasticity for any move at any margin. Four break-evens for Kettle's tin. And a one-sentence decision rule: act on the move that wins across the whole range; if none does, the next step is to buy information, not to pick the most convenient point.

Part 1 · covers the elasticity as an input that arrives as a range

The number you are handed is a range

5 min live

Price elasticity of demand, in one line: the percent change in units sold for a 1% change in price. An elasticity of -1.8 says a 1% price rise loses about 1.8% of units. Estimating it from sales data is Economics' job, and it is harder than it looks, because prices and demand move together for reasons that are not the price. See why the naive number lies and the elasticity bench. An honest estimate comes with a standard error, so what reaches the pricing team is a range.

LiveAsk for the range, not the point3 min▶

What to request. The estimate, its standard error, and the method. Economics reports two estimates on real Olist rows: a within-product estimate of -1.788 (se 0.149) and a two-way estimate of -1.854 (se 0.150). Plus or minus two standard errors around -1.854 gives -1.554 to -2.154. That bracket is the honest input.

Why the point is not enough. A point estimate of -1.854 invites the question "what does a 5% rise do at -1.854?", which has one answer. The better question is "what does a 5% rise do anywhere from -1.554 to -2.154?", which may have two answers with opposite signs. If it does, the point estimate is carrying the decision, and the point is the least reliable thing you were given.

What you do not do. Re-run the regression with a different control until the number suits the plan. Estimation belongs to the people who own the method; if you doubt the range, take that to them with the reason.

Self-studyWhose elasticity is it?3 min▶

Whose products. The Olist estimates come from marketplace products sold in Brazil, many sellers, many categories. Kettle's tin is one brand's product with its own loyal buyers. A borrowed elasticity is a starting range, not a measurement of your product; say so in the deck.

What horizon. Monthly data says how units moved within months. A customer who stocks up before a rise, or drifts away over a year, is a different elasticity. Ask which horizon the estimate speaks to.

Over what price range. An elasticity is estimated over the price moves that actually happened in the data. If those moves were mostly a few percent, a 10% move asks the number to travel further than its evidence. Read the elasticity bench for how the estimate was built before you lean on it.

Real world

Two honest numbers, one bracket. On real Olist marketplace rows (Brazilian e-commerce, delivered orders, prices in BRL), Economics' within-product and two-way estimates land 0.066 apart: -1.788 and -1.854, each with a standard error near 0.15. The two methods agree far more closely than either is precise, which is why the pricing question has to be asked of the whole bracket, not of whichever number is quoted.

Part 2 · covers constant-elasticity arithmetic and the break-even elasticity

Where each move stops paying

5 min live

With the elasticity as an input, a price move becomes three lines of arithmetic. Under constant elasticity, new units = units x (1 + move) ^ elasticity. Profit change = (new price - cost) x new units - (price - cost) x units. Solve for the elasticity where the profit change is zero and you get the break-even elasticity for that move: at contribution margin m and move x, it is ln(m / (m + x)) / ln(1 + x). A price rise pays while demand is less elastic than its break-even.

LiveThe break-even elasticity4 min▶

Kettle's tin. Price 1,800 yen, unit cost 720 yen, contribution margin (1,800 - 720) / 1,800 = 60%, and 10,000 tins a month, so contribution before any move is 1,080 x 10,000 = 10,800,000 yen a month. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

One move, worked. A +5% rise takes the price to 1,890 yen. At an elasticity of -1.2, units = 10,000 x 1.05 ^ -1.2 = 9,431.33 tins (rounded). Profit = (1,890 - 720) x 9,431.33 = 11,034,656 yen, against 10,800,000 before: about +234,656. The widget, unrounded, prints +234,654.

★ Break-even elasticities, m = 60%move break-even reading -10% -1.73 a cut pays only if demand is MORE elastic than -1.73 -5% -1.70 a cut pays only if demand is MORE elastic than -1.70 +5% -1.64 a rise pays while demand is LESS elastic than -1.64 +10% -1.62 a rise pays while demand is LESS elastic than -1.62

Reading it. All four break-evens sit in a narrow band, -1.62 to -1.73. For a 60% margin product, the question "raise or cut?" turns on whether demand is more or less elastic than about -1.6 to -1.7. Any range that crosses that band cannot settle it.

Self-studyWhy the formula is a log3 min▶

Write price p, cost c, margin m = (p - c) / p. After a move x, profit per unit is p(1 + x) - c = p(m + x), and units are q(1 + x) ^ e. Profit is unchanged when p(m + x) x q(1 + x) ^ e = p m q, which simplifies to (1 + x) ^ e = m / (m + x). Take logs of both sides: e = ln(m / (m + x)) / ln(1 + x). The log appears because the elasticity sits in an exponent.

★ One worked check, +5% at m = 60%break-even e = ln(0.60 / 0.65) / ln(1.05) = -1.64 check: 1.05 ^ -1.64 x 0.65 / 0.60 = 0.92310 x 1.08333 = 1.00003, about 1: profit unchanged

The tiny gap from exactly 1 is the rounding of -1.6406 to -1.64. The same formula at a 40% margin gives a different set of break-evens: margin is half the answer, which is why Business session 1 kept asking "at what margin?"

Real world

The cut that needed 12,000 tins. Kettle's promotions lead proposed a permanent 10% cut on the tin "because tea buyers are price sensitive". The analyst replied with one number: the break-even for -10% is -1.73. Check: 0.9 ^ -1.73 = 1.20, so the tin would need about 12,000 tins a month at 1,620 yen just to earn the same 10,800,000 yen, the same 20.0% rise Business session 1 derived from d / (m - d). The question changed from "are buyers price sensitive?" to "is there evidence they are more elastic than -1.73?" Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Part 3 · covers the decision that survives the range vs the one that needs the point

The verdict that holds across the range

5 min live

Put the range next to the break-evens. If a move's break-even sits outside the range, the move either wins or loses wherever the truth is: that decision survives the range. If the break-even sits inside, the sign of the profit change depends on where in the range the truth sits, and the decision rests on the point estimate. A narrower range helps only if it does not straddle a break-even.

LiveThree ranges, three answers4 min▶

Handed range, -1.2 to -2.4. Every break-even, -1.62 to -1.73, sits inside. A +5% rise makes +234,654 yen a month at -1.2 and loses 392,856 at -2.4. Every move depends on the point estimate.

Narrower range, -1.2 to -1.5. The whole range is less elastic than -1.62. Both rises win at every elasticity in it; both cuts lose at every elasticity in it. This is the only range of the three that supports a decision on its own.

Economics' Olist range, -1.554 to -2.154. Narrower than the handed range, built on real rows, and still useless for this decision: it straddles all four break-evens. A +5% rise is positive at the low end and negative at the middle and the high end. Precision is not the same as decisiveness.

The rule. Compare the range to the break-evens, not to zero and not to the point estimate. If no break-even falls inside, act. If one does, the decision is not yet made.

Self-studyWhen no move survives3 min▶

Buy information. When the range straddles a break-even, the most valuable next step is evidence about your own product near your own price. A controlled price test does that: a share of customers or stores sees the new price, the rest see the old one, and the difference in units is your elasticity, measured where you will use it.

Design it properly. How many customers, for how long, and how to read the result belong to the people who own test method. Use Experimentation's A/B test session for the design. What the pricing team brings is the target: the test only has to tell you which side of -1.64 the tin sits on, which is a much smaller question than "what is the elasticity?"

Or choose the smaller bet. If a test is not possible, size the downside. At -2.4, the far end of the handed range, a +10% move loses 776,299 yen a month and a +5% move 392,856. The smaller move is the cheaper way to be wrong while you watch the units.

Real world

The "raise 10%" deck. Kettle's planning team brought a proposal to raise the tin 10%, with a projected gain built on a single elasticity in a footnote. The analyst asked for the range behind it and got -1.2 to -2.4. The break-even for +10% is -1.62, inside the range. At -1.2 the move earns +438,266 yen a month; at -2.4 it loses 776,299. The deck's gain was real only if the footnote's point was right, and nothing in the deck said why it should be. The decision became "run a price test on a share of the web shop first". Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Kettle's break-even elasticity for a +5% rise is -1.64. What does that mean?

The break-even is a threshold computed from the margin and the move, not an estimate of demand. It tells you which side of the line the true elasticity has to be on for the move to pay.

2 · Which range lets a +5% rise survive the whole range, and why?

What matters is whether the range straddles the break-even. The Olist range is narrower than the handed one and real, yet it still crosses -1.64, so the sign of the profit change depends on the point.

3 · Why is the elasticity never re-estimated before the decision?

Economics owns the estimate and its standard error. The pricing analyst owns the arithmetic from range to decision, and takes doubts about the range back to the people who built it.
