Where the money leaks

The price on the list is not the price you keep. Between the list and the bank sit volume discounts, promotional allowances, rebates, early-payment discounts, co-op marketing and freight nobody charged for, and most of them never appear on the invoice. Tonight follows one bag of tea down that cascade, shows why one product ends up selling at dozens of different prices, and asks the question most companies cannot answer: who owns the total? Kettle & Co's wholesale arm is constructed; the waterfall is the one Analyst session 1 builds from its rows.

Part 0

Where we start

Business session 1 showed that a price move lands on profit whole, and Business session 2 asked where the price should come from. Both assumed the price is the price. Tonight drops that assumption. Kettle & Co, a constructed tea brand in Japan, sells a 1 kg catering bag to 40 wholesale accounts (cafes, grocers and hotels) at a list price of 9,600 yen. Every one of those accounts gets something off: some on the invoice, most after it. We follow the yen down, read the spread across accounts, and end with an audit you can run on your own business tomorrow.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A way to read any price as a waterfall from list to pocket, the habit of asking for the pocket price band instead of the average discount, and a filled leak audit: every concession you give, whether it is on or off the invoice, who owns it, who approves it, and the one leak to put under a pocket price rule first.

Part 1 · covers the pocket price waterfall

From list price to pocket price

5 min live

Marn and Rosiello named it in Harvard Business Review in 1992: revenue cascades from the list price down to the invoice price and then down again to the pocket price, what the seller actually keeps from the transaction. They called the picture the pocket price waterfall and argued that pocket price, not invoice price, is the right measure of a deal. In their flooring example, the drop from invoice to pocket alone was 22.7%.

LiveReading the cascade3 min▶

The list. Kettle lists the 1 kg catering bag at 9,600 yen. Nobody pays that.

On the invoice. A volume discount takes 629 yen a bag and a promotional allowance 97, so the invoice reads 8,874 yen, 92.4% of list. This is the number most sales reports call "price".

After the invoice. Four more items leave later: a volume rebate paid at the end of the period (116), a discount for paying early (104), co-op marketing money for the account's own promotions (275), and freight Kettle pays but never bills (714). Together, 1,209 yen a bag.

The pocket. The waterfall in Analyst session 1 prints the bottom line: "Pocket price 7,665 yen a bag: 79.8% of list. Off-invoice leakage 1,209 yen, on-invoice 726 yen." The off-invoice share is the larger one, and it is the one the invoice cannot see.

These are unit-weighted averages across 3,812 bags, so a big account counts for more than a small one. A simple average across the 40 accounts would weigh every account equally and give a different number; ask which one you are shown.

Self-studyWhat else falls off the waterfall2 min▶

Kettle's cascade has six steps because the constructed rows hold six. A real business usually has more, and the ones that hide best are the ones nobody books as a price: returns accepted without a restocking charge, free samples and tasting stock handed to an account, card and payment platform fees, slotting or listing fees a retailer charges to stock the product, extended payment terms (money you wait for is money you finance), and the cost of special packing or labelling done for one customer. None of them have numbers here, and that is the point: list them before you estimate them. Marn and Rosiello's own list of off-invoice items names prompt-payment discounts, volume bonuses, cooperative advertising and freight; the rest is yours to add.

Real world

The flooring example. In Marn and Rosiello's flooring example, the pocket price sat 22.7% below the invoice price: almost a quarter of what the invoice said never reached the seller. That is why they argue for pocket price, not invoice price, as the measure of a deal. Kettle's constructed drop from invoice to pocket is smaller (1,209 of 8,874, about 13.6%, our arithmetic), but the shape is the same: the bigger leak sits below the line the report stops at.

Part 2 · covers the pocket price band

One bag, forty pocket prices

5 min live

An average pocket price hides the most useful fact of all: one product sells at many pocket prices. Marn and Rosiello called the spread the pocket price band. In their flooring example the highest and lowest transactions were 35% apart, and across the industries they studied they saw bands of 60% in lighting, 70% in computer peripherals, 200% in specialty chemicals and 500% at a fastener supplier. A wide band is not automatically bad; an unexplained one is.

LiveWhy the band is wider than the invoice3 min▶

Two bands, one product. The waterfall in Analyst session 1 prints: "Pocket band 6,371 yen to 8,986 yen (2,615 yen wide); invoice band 8,498 yen to 9,600 yen (1,102 yen wide)." On the invoice, Kettle's accounts look like they pay roughly the same. In the pocket, they do not.

Why it widens. Off-invoice items are not spread evenly. Freight depends on drop size and distance, co-op money goes to whoever asked for it, rebates go to whoever hit a threshold. Each one is a separate decision, and they stack on different accounts.

The volume trap. You would expect the biggest buyers to sit lowest. On average they do: the 5 largest accounts average 7,402 yen, the 5 smallest 8,328. But the account at 6,626 yen is a cafe buying 49 bags, sitting right next to a cafe buying 164 bags at 6,371, while a grocer buying 47 bags pays the most of anyone, 8,986. Size does not explain the 49-bag cafe. Something else does, and nobody has asked.

Self-studyA band is a list of conversations2 min▶

Read the band as a to-do list, not a chart. The accounts at the bottom are the ones to understand first: is the low pocket price paying for something real (volume, a long contract, cheap delivery, a strategic logo) or did it drift there one concession at a time? The accounts at the top are worth a second look too: a high pocket price can be loyalty, or an account that will leave when a competitor calls. The 35% to 500% they report is a range across very different industries, not a benchmark for yours; the useful comparison is your band against your own explanation of it.

Real world

The 49-bag cafe. Kettle's account manager remembered the reason as soon as the dot was pointed out: two years earlier the cafe had agreed to feature Kettle's tea on its menu board, and in return got co-op money and free delivery on every order, however small. The menu board came down eighteen months ago. The concessions did not. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Part 3 · covers who owns each leak and why invoice reports hide them

Four budgets, nobody sees the total

5 min live

The leaks are not hidden by accident. Each off-invoice item is paid out of a different budget: rebates from sales, co-op money from marketing, early-payment discounts from finance, absorbed freight from logistics. Each owner sees one slice, as a cost line, and none of them sees the pocket price of a single account. The fix is not a new system; it is one report and one rule.

LiveOne owner for the pocket price3 min▶

Why invoice reports hide it. The invoice is where the price is recorded, so the sales report reads the invoice. Everything after it is booked as a cost in someone else's budget: a rebate accrual in sales, a promotion line in marketing, a discount in finance, a carriage line in logistics. Kettle's invoice view prints exactly this blind spot when the widget's invoice-only switch is on: "Invoice view: price realisation 92.4%. The 1,209 yen a bag that leaves after the invoice is invisible here."

The report. One row per account: list, invoice, every off-invoice item allocated back to the account, pocket. Analyst session 1 builds exactly that from order rows.

The rule. Any off-invoice concession above a threshold (say, more than a set number of yen a bag, or a set share of the account's revenue) is approved by someone who can see that account's pocket price at the moment of signing. The threshold is a business choice; the point is that the person saying yes sees what the yes costs.

The owner. Somebody has to own the pocket price as a number, usually a commercial or pricing lead, so that a freight decision in logistics and a co-op decision in marketing land on the same desk.

Self-studyPrice realisation as a board number2 min▶

Price realisation is pocket price over list price. Kettle's is 79.8%: for every 100 yen on the price list, about 80 reach the bank. It is one number a board can track every quarter, and it moves for reasons a board should hear about: a new rebate scheme, a freight contract, a key account renegotiation. Two cautions. First, the invoice version (92.4% for Kettle) is the one most systems produce by default, and it flatters; ask which one you are shown. Second, a falling realisation is not automatically bad: a deliberate move into a lower-priced channel can be worth it. The board number is a prompt for the question, not the answer.

Real world

The freight on small hotel drops. Kettle's logistics lead had a target for cost per delivery, and hit it. What nobody had joined up: hotels ordering two or three bags at a time to a city-centre loading dock were each getting a delivery Kettle paid for in full and never billed. Logistics saw a carriage line within budget; sales saw hotel invoices at near list. Only the pocket view showed that freight was the biggest single leak in the waterfall, 714 yen of the 1,209 off invoice, concentrated on the smallest drops. The fix was a minimum order for free delivery, signed off by the commercial lead with the pocket report open. Constructed Kettle & Co example. (Kettle's office-tea arm, which sells to companies through a sales pipeline rather than a price list, has its own story in Sales Pipeline Analytics ↗.)

Build-along

The leak audit

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

No code, one sheet. Use your own business, or Kettle's wholesale arm. Five steps take you from "we give a few discounts" to a ranked list with one leak chosen for a pocket price rule. Do step 1 together, steps 2 to 4 in pairs, then each pair reads out its step 5 choice and why.

Step 1 · List every price concession you give. Everything that makes the money you keep smaller than the list price: discounts, rebates, allowances, free freight, payment terms, samples, returns, fees you absorb. Brainstorm wide; a concession nobody calls a price is still one.

★ Step 1 · the listconcessions we give: 1 ____________ 2 ____________ 3 ____________ 4 ____________ 5 ____________ 6 ____________ Kettle (constructed): volume discount · promo allowance · volume rebate early-payment discount · co-op marketing · freight absorbed

Step 2 · Mark each one on or off the invoice. On-invoice items reduce the price the customer sees on the bill. Off-invoice items are paid or absorbed later and live in a cost line. Count how many sit off.

★ Step 2 · on or offconcession on / off ____________ ____ ____________ ____ Kettle: on = volume discount, promo allowance (726 yen a bag) off = rebate, early payment, co-op, freight (1,209 yen a bag)

Step 3 · Name the owner and the approver. The owner is the budget it is paid from. The approver is the person who says yes to a new or bigger one. If the answer is "it just happens", write "nobody": that is the most important cell on the sheet.

★ Step 3 · whoconcession owner (budget) approver ____________ ____________ ____________ Kettle freight: owner logistics, approver nobody (free delivery on every order) Kettle co-op: owner marketing, approver brand manager

Step 4 · Estimate yen per unit. Divide the year's total for each concession by the units sold. Rough is fine; an order of magnitude beats a blank. If you can, note which accounts get the most.

★ Step 4 · how muchconcession yearly total ÷ units = per unit ____________ ________ ÷ ______ = ______ Kettle (per bag, as printed in Analyst session 1): freight 714 · co-op 275 · rebate 116 · early payment 104 · promo 97 · volume discount 629

Step 5 · Rank and pick one. Rank by yen per unit, then look at the approver column. Pick the biggest leak that nobody approves, and write the pocket price rule for it in one sentence.

★ Step 5 · the rule"Our biggest unapproved leak is ___ at ___ per unit. From now on, any ___ above ___ is approved by ___, who sees the account's pocket price before signing." Kettle: "Our biggest unapproved leak is freight at 714 yen a bag. From now on, free delivery on orders under a minimum is approved by the commercial lead, who sees the account's pocket price before signing."

Keep the sheet. The volume discount (629 yen) is bigger than three of the off-invoice items, but it is on the invoice and has an approver already. The audit is not about the biggest number; it is about the biggest number nobody is looking at.

Homework

Before Business session 4

30 min
  1. Finish the leak audit for one product line, with a yen (or dollar) figure per unit for every row, even a rough one.
  2. Ask finance for your price realisation: the invoice version and, if anyone can produce it, the pocket version. Bring both, or bring the fact that only one exists.
  3. Find your lowest-pocket account for one product and write down why it sits there. If nobody knows, that is the finding.
  4. Draft the approval rule for your rank-1 leak: the threshold, the approver, and the report they would need open when signing.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Business session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What is the difference between Kettle's invoice price (8,874 yen) and its pocket price (7,665 yen)?

Invoice price is what the bill says; pocket price is what the seller keeps after the off-invoice items, 1,209 yen a bag for Kettle. Marn and Rosiello argue the pocket price is the right measure.

2 · Kettle's pocket band is 2,615 yen wide; its invoice band is 1,102 yen wide. What does that tell you?

Off-invoice items land unevenly, so the spread more than doubles after the invoice. And size does not explain it all: a 49-bag cafe sits near the bottom while a 47-bag grocer is at the top.

3 · Why do off-invoice leaks so often go unseen?

Rebates sit in sales, co-op in marketing, early payment in finance, freight in logistics. The fix is a pocket price report per account and an approver who can see it.
